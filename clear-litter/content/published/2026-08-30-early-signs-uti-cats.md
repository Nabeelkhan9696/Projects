<p>Cats hide illness. It's instinct, and it's why urinary problems &mdash; among the most common and most treatable feline health issues &mdash; so often go unnoticed until they're advanced. The earliest signs almost all show up in one place you already look every day: the litter box. Here are the seven signs worth knowing, roughly in the order owners tend to notice them.</p>

<h2>What are the early signs of a UTI in cats?</h2>
<p>The earliest signs are changes in litter box behavior &mdash; more frequent trips producing less urine, straining or vocalizing in the box, urinating outside the box, blood-tinged urine, excessive genital grooming, a stronger or unusual urine smell, and general behavior changes like hiding. Any one of them is a reason to watch closely; two or more together are a reason to call your vet.</p>

<h3>1. Frequent trips, tiny results</h3>
<p>A cat with an irritated urinary tract feels constant urgency. You'll see more visits to the box but smaller wet spots &mdash; in crystal litter, lots of small patches instead of the usual few larger ones. This is often the very first sign, and it's easy to shrug off as "drinking more."</p>

<h3>2. Straining or crying in the box</h3>
<p>Posturing for a long time with little produced, or meowing while urinating, means urination hurts. <strong>Important:</strong> a male cat straining and producing <em>nothing</em> may have a urethral blockage &mdash; that is a life-threatening emergency measured in hours, not days. Go to a vet immediately.</p>

<h3>3. Peeing outside the box &mdash; the one owners miss</h3>
<p>This is the sign most often misread as a behavior problem. A cat that suddenly pees on the bathmat or in the sink isn't being spiteful &mdash; cats in pain associate the box itself with the pain and look for somewhere that "won't hurt." If box avoidance appears without any change in litter, box, or household, think medical first, behavioral second. (If you've ruled out illness, our <a href="/blogs/news/crystal-litter-multiple-cats">multi-cat setup guide</a> covers the common behavioral causes.)</p>

<h3>4. Pink, red, or cloudy urine</h3>
<p>Blood in urine is easy to miss in clay litter, where everything turns the same dark clump color. On white silica crystals it's unmistakable &mdash; one advantage of a light-colored litter bed. Any pink or red tint is a same-week vet visit; visible blood plus straining is a same-day one.</p>

<h3>5. Excessive licking</h3>
<p>Cats groom pain. Repeated, focused licking of the urinary opening &mdash; especially right after leaving the box &mdash; is self-soothing for irritation you can't see.</p>

<h3>6. A stronger or different smell</h3>
<p>Infected urine often smells stronger, sharper, or just <em>different</em>. If the box smells worse on the same cleaning schedule with the same litter, don't assume the litter failed &mdash; smell change plus any other sign on this list points at the cat, not the box. (Our <a href="/blogs/news/house-smells-like-cat-litter">litter smell guide</a> covers how to tell maintenance odor from a warning sign.)</p>

<h3>7. Hiding and irritability</h3>
<p>The vaguest sign, and the one that means "already uncomfortable." A normally social cat that starts hiding, or a mellow cat that gets snappy when picked up, is telling you something hurts somewhere &mdash; combined with any box change, urinary is a prime suspect.</p>

<h2>What to do if you spot the signs</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>One mild sign:</strong> watch closely for 24&ndash;48 hours, count box trips, and photograph anything odd (vets love photos of weird urine, truly).</li>
<li><strong>Two or more signs, or blood:</strong> book a vet visit this week; same-day if the cat seems distressed.</li>
<li><strong>Male cat straining with no urine:</strong> emergency vet, now. Blockage kills within a day or two.</li>
<li><strong>Don't home-treat.</strong> Cranberry supplements and internet remedies delay real treatment; "UTI symptoms" in cats are frequently stress cystitis or crystals, which need different care &mdash; only a urine test can tell.</li>
</ul>

<h2>FAQ</h2>
<p><strong>Can a cat UTI go away on its own?</strong><br>
Don't count on it. Some stress-related cystitis eases with environmental changes, but a true bacterial infection needs antibiotics, and blocked male cats are a medical emergency. Since the symptoms look identical from the outside, a vet urine test is the only reliable way to tell.</p>
<p><strong>What does cat urine smell like with a UTI?</strong><br>
Often stronger and sharper than usual — sometimes described as more ammonia-like or simply "off." A smell change on an unchanged cleaning routine, paired with any behavior change, is a good reason to call your vet.</p>
<p><strong>Why is my cat peeing outside the litter box all of a sudden?</strong><br>
Sudden box avoidance with no change in litter or household is a medical red flag before it's a behavior problem — cats in urinary pain blame the box. Rule out a UTI, crystals, or cystitis with a vet first, then troubleshoot behavior.</p>
<p><strong>Are male or female cats more prone to urinary problems?</strong><br>
Females get bacterial UTIs somewhat more often, but males have the dangerous version: their narrower urethra can block completely. A male cat straining without producing urine needs an emergency vet immediately.</p>
