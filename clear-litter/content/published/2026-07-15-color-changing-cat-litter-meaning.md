You scooped the box, and some of the crystals are a different color than
they were yesterday. Is that bad? Here's the framework: **health-monitoring
litter has two kinds of indicators — one that reacts to urine pH and one
that reacts to traces of blood — and a color change means "look closer,"
not "panic."** This guide covers what each type of change can signal, and
the exact steps to take when you see one.

One note before we start: match what you see against the **color guide
printed on your bag** — that's the authoritative key for your litter. This
article explains what the categories of change mean and what to do about
them.

## How do color-changing crystals work?

Our [health monitoring litter](/products/health-monitoring-cat-litter) is
silica crystal litter with indicators built into the beads. Because silica
absorbs urine directly into the crystal, the indicators react to what's
chemically *in* the urine:

- **pH indicators** respond when urine is unusually alkaline or acidic.
- **Blood indicators** respond to traces of blood that are usually
  invisible against ordinary litter.

Your cat uses the box normally; you glance at the crystals when you scoop.
No test strips, no sample collection, no cooperation required from the cat
— which anyone who has tried to get a urine sample from a cat will
appreciate.

## What does a pH-related change mean?

Healthy cat urine sits in a fairly narrow pH band. When it drifts:

- **Too alkaline** is associated with urinary tract infections and with
  struvite crystal formation (a common type of bladder crystal/stone).
- **Too acidic** is associated with a different stone type (calcium
  oxalate) and can accompany metabolic issues.

Diet can nudge pH too — a food change, new treats, or prescription diets
can shift readings without illness. That's why the pattern matters more
than a single reading: a one-off shift after you switched foods is
unremarkable; a persistent shift over a day or two is worth a vet call.

## What does a blood-indicator change mean?

Blood in urine (hematuria) is never noise — it's the more urgent of the
two signals. Common causes include UTIs, cystitis (bladder inflammation,
often stress-related in cats), bladder stones, and kidney issues. The
amounts involved are often far too small to see with the naked eye, which
is exactly why this indicator earns its place in the box.

If the blood indicator shows and your cat is also straining, crying in the
box, or making frequent tiny trips — **especially a male cat** — treat it
as urgent and call your vet the same day. Those signs together can mean a
developing blockage, which is an emergency.

## What should you do when you see a color change?

1. **Don't panic — verify.** Note which indicator changed (check your
   bag's color guide) and take a photo of the litter.
2. **Watch the pattern for 24–48 hours** — unless the blood indicator
   showed or your cat is showing symptoms, in which case skip to step 4.
3. **Note behavior:** frequency of box visits, amount, straining, hiding,
   appetite, water intake. These observations multiply the value of the
   litter's signal.
4. **Call your vet** if the change repeats, if blood is indicated, or if
   any behavior looks off. Bring the photo — "the litter flagged elevated
   pH on Tuesday and Wednesday, and she's visiting the box more" is a
   genuinely useful history for a vet.

## What color changes DON'T mean

The indicators are a **smoke alarm, not a diagnosis**. They can't tell you
*which* condition is present — only a urinalysis at the clinic can. Two
other things that aren't alerts:

- **Gradual yellowing across the whole box** is normal saturation — the
  crystals reaching the end of their working life, not a health signal.
  (More on that in our
  [guide to how long crystal litter lasts](/blogs/news/how-long-does-crystal-cat-litter-last).)
- **A single, isolated odd patch** right after a diet change is usually
  the diet, not disease. Verify against the pattern, per the steps above.

## How to get reliable readings

- **Stir the crystals daily** when you scoop, so urine spreads evenly and
  indicators contact fresh beads.
- **Keep depth around 2 inches** and replace the litter every 3–4 weeks —
  saturated crystals can't signal cleanly.
- **One box per cat (plus one)** in multi-cat homes, so you know whose
  chemistry you're reading.
- **Learn your baseline.** After the first week you'll know what normal
  looks like for your cat, and anomalies will jump out at a glance.

## FAQ

**How accurate is color-changing cat litter?**
It's an early-warning screen, not a diagnostic test. It reliably flags the
chemistry changes it's designed for (pH shifts, traces of blood), but only
a veterinary urinalysis can tell you what's causing them.

**The whole box slowly turned yellowish — is my cat sick?**
Probably not. Uniform, gradual yellowing is normal crystal saturation and
means it's time for a fresh bag. Health alerts look different — check the
color guide on your bag.

**Which cats benefit most from health-monitoring litter?**
Cats with a urinary history, senior cats (7+), male cats (highest blockage
risk), and any home that wants unscented litter anyway — this is the
unscented formula in our lineup.

**Does it work with multiple cats?**
Yes, but a shared box tells you *a* cat has an issue, not which one. Use
one box per cat plus one, or separate cats temporarily after an alert.
